In plain English

Your engine is already run by software

Every modern engine has a small computer deciding how much fuel to inject, when to light it, and how hard the turbo blows. A remap rewrites those decisions for your specific vehicle.

Manufacturers set them conservatively because one file has to cope with poor fuel in one country, extreme heat in another, and owners who skip services. On most engines that leaves real headroom — and it's yours already. No parts come off, nothing is cut out, and it can be put back exactly as it was.

Straight answers

Questions we get asked most

If yours isn't here, ring and ask. You'll get a straight answer even when it's not the one you were hoping for.

Is it legal?
Yes. Remapping is legal in the UK. You must declare it to your insurer and we give you the details in writing to pass on. What isn't legal is removing emissions equipment from a road vehicle, which is why we don't do it.
Will it affect my insurance?
It has to be declared, like any modification. Most insurers handle it without much fuss; an undeclared remap is the fastest way to have a claim refused.
What about my warranty?
If the vehicle is still under manufacturer warranty, assume a remap affects it. We'll talk it through honestly before you book rather than after, and the original file is archived so it can go back before a dealer visit.
Can it be undone?
Yes. Your factory file is read off and stored against your registration before anything is written, so the vehicle can be returned to exactly how it left the factory.
Will it use more fuel?
Driven the same way, usually slightly less, because there's more torque low down. Driven to enjoy the extra power, obviously more.
Does anything get replaced?
Not on a standard remap. No parts come off and nothing is cut. The car looks identical when you collect it.

Not sure which one you need?

Tell us the vehicle and what you want out of it. If a remap isn't the right answer, that's what you'll be told.

The complete explanation

What is a remap, really?

No jargon, no sales pitch. If you've been quoted for one and want to understand what you're actually buying, this is the page.

Start here: your engine is a computer decision, thousands of times a second

Every engine built in the last twenty-odd years has a control unit — the ECU. It reads what the pedal is doing, how fast the engine is turning, how much air is coming in and how hot everything is, then decides how much fuel to inject, exactly when to ignite it, and how hard the turbo should blow.

Those decisions aren't calculated from scratch each time. They're looked up from tables of pre-set values written at the factory. A remap means rewriting those tables.

The six questions everybody actually asks

If there's spare performance, why didn't the manufacturer use it?

Because they write one file for the whole world. It has to survive poor fuel in one country and forty-degree heat in another, cope with owners who never service the car, and hit an emissions and tax bracket that suits the sales brochure. They also want the cheaper model in the range to stay slower than the expensive one — which is why two cars with the same engine often leave the factory with different power figures. Building for one vehicle, in one climate, driven by one person, leaves genuine headroom.

Does anything physically change on the car?

No. On a standard remap nothing is removed, cut, drilled or replaced. We connect to the diagnostic socket — the same one a garage plugs into — read the software off, rewrite it, and write it back. The car looks exactly the same when you collect it.

Is it safe for the engine?

It depends entirely on who writes the file. The manufacturer builds in margins: how hot things are allowed to get, how much pressure a cylinder can take, how lean the mixture is permitted to run. A good file moves fuel, air and timing together and leaves those margins alone. A bad file buys a bigger headline number by spending them — and it will still drive fine for a while, which is what makes it dangerous. That's the whole difference, and it's invisible from the driver's seat.

Can it be undone?

Yes, and this matters more than people realise. Before anything is changed we read your original factory file off and archive it against your registration. It can be put back at any point — before a dealer visit, before selling the car, or simply because you changed your mind.

What about insurance and warranty?

Insurance: declare it. It's a modification and an undeclared one can invalidate a claim, so we give you the specifics in writing to forward straight to your insurer. Most policies take it without drama. Warranty: if the vehicle is still under manufacturer cover, assume a remap affects it — we'll say so plainly before you book, and the archived original means it can go back to standard first.

Remap or tuning box?

A tuning box sits between the ECU and the engine and fakes the signals going past — the ECU thinks one thing is happening while something else actually is. A remap changes the decisions at source, which is more precise and lets us respect the safety limits rather than work around them. Boxes have one advantage: they unplug. If that matters more to you than quality of result, we'll tell you honestly.

How to judge a tuner

What separates a good file from a bad one

Anyone can buy the tool that loads a file. It takes twenty minutes and the car can't tell the difference. These are the questions worth asking whoever you use — including us.

"Is the file written for my car, or bought off a shelf?"

Same badge, very different story underneath. Mileage, service history, turbo condition and previous work all change what's safe to ask for.

"Will you log it before and after?"

Requested against actual boost, fuel trims, timing pulled, exhaust temperature. Without measurement, nobody knows what the file is really doing.

"Do you keep my original?"

If the answer is anything other than an immediate yes, walk away.

"What won't you do?"

A tuner who'll do anything you ask, including the illegal things, is telling you exactly how much care goes into the rest of it.

Stages

"Stage" isn't a power figure. It's a list of parts.

We match the file to the hardware that's actually on the car, never the other way round.

S1

Stage 1 — standard car

Nothing changed under the bonnet. Everything gained comes from calibration within what the standard turbo, injectors, clutch and cooling will support. This is the right answer for the large majority of cars.

S2

Stage 2 — supporting mods

Intake, exhaust or intercooler already fitted. The file is written around what's genuinely on the vehicle, so bring the spec list when you book.

BSP

Bespoke

Track use, heavy towing, a specific rev range, or a car modified beyond the usual. Priced after we've seen it and logged it, not before.

Drivability

A car that's quicker but worse to drive is a failed job

Power that arrives in a lump is easy to write and unpleasant to live with. Getting a curve that pulls cleanly from low down and keeps pulling takes far longer, and it's the part you'll notice every single day.

Pedal response, part-throttle behaviour, how it holds a gear on a hill, whether it's still smooth pulling out of a junction cold. None of it shows on a dyno printout. All of it shows on the drive home.

The mechanism

Nothing gets starved or softened

Economy work isn't about weakening the engine. It's about torque arriving earlier, so you use less throttle to hold the same speed and change up sooner.

Which is why we're upfront about the catch: the gain is only real if your driving lets it be. Short cold journeys, heavy traffic and a heavy right foot will cancel it out entirely. If your mileage doesn't justify the work, we'll say so.

Torque, earlier

Stronger pull from low revs so you short-shift naturally and sit in a higher gear at motorway speed.

Smoother part-throttle

Cruising stops needing constant small corrections, which is where a surprising amount of diesel goes.

Fewer downshifts

Hills and overtakes stop demanding a gear you didn't want.

Nothing removed

The economics

Cheaper than the horsepower you'd have to buy

Machinery replacement costs have pushed most farms into keeping tractors far longer than they used to. A remap lets a machine you already own pull a wider implement or cover more acres in a day — at a fraction of what the next size up would cost you.

That's the whole argument. Everything else on this page is detail.

What changes on a machine

Torque that holds

Pulling power that stays with you through a long pass instead of tailing off as everything heats up. On heavy draft work that difference is measured in hours.

Steadier PTO work

Engine speed held more consistently as load varies, so driven implements run evenly rather than surging and dropping.

Fewer trips to the bowser

Better efficiency in the working band means more hours between fills and less of the day spent driving back to the yard.

Written to the driveline

The transmission is usually the real ceiling, not the engine. Adding torque a box can't hold is an expensive way to save money on fuel.

Emissions systems untouched

No DPF, EGR or AdBlue deletes on machinery either. It stays compliant, and just as importantly it stays sellable.

Our story

My grandfather never touched a laptop in his life

He'd have had no interest in one. He also had a rule about explaining your own work that this business still runs on, decades later, on equipment he wouldn't recognise.

A workshop, a grandfather, and a boy who wouldn't stay out of the way

Long before any of this involved a laptop, there was an engine on a bench, a grandfather working on it, and a grandson who was supposed to be keeping out from under his feet and never once did.

You learn things in a workshop that you can't learn from a manual. That a noise tells you more than a fault code. That the customer standing in the doorway deserves a straight answer even when it isn't the one they want. That the cheap fix always comes back, usually at the worst possible moment and usually costing more than doing it properly would have in the first place.

None of that has anything to do with software. All of it decides how a remap gets written.

"If you can't explain what you changed and why you changed it, you had no business touching it."

— The rule this workshop still runs on

Then and now

From spanners to software

Engines stopped being purely mechanical. The adjustments that used to be made with a screwdriver moved into tables inside a control unit. The thinking behind them didn't change at all.

You still have to know what the engine is doing, why it's doing it, and where the limit sits before you go anywhere near it. The screwdriver became a laptop. The judgement is identical, and it's still the part that takes years rather than weeks to build.

That's the whole business, really. Same trade, different instrument — now applied to cars, vans and farm machinery across Shropshire, with the same rule holding for all three.
